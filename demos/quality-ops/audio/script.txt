Here's how I think about it.

Quality isn't a phase you bolt on at the end of a sprint.
It's how the whole queue moves.
Same idea as keeping guests flowing through a gate on a busy Saturday.

I'd lead that rethink by doing the work myself.
Not by writing another policy doc.

First, I own the signal.
If the suite is flaky, I go fix it before I ask anyone for more coverage.
A red build nobody trusts just teaches people to ship around quality.
I've kept daily continuous integration above ninety percent by killing brittle waits, and making a failure mean something again.

Second, I show up earlier.
Vague stories are where bugs are born.
So I sit in refinement, help write acceptance criteria, and pair on the hard paths while the change is still cheap.
Not after the pull request is already stamped done.

Third, I make the good path the fast path.
Shared fixtures, shared libraries, and gap checks that point at real risk.
People take whatever shortcut is quickest.
If solid tests are slower than clicking around by hand, you lose that fight.

Where I'd push in the process is intake and contracts first.
On a platform with tickets, point of sale, partners, and payments, things usually break at the boundaries.
Then the pipeline.
It has to be quick and trusted, or folks route around it.
And release isn't a blame meeting.
It's a feedback loop into the next story.

What actually changes how a team behaves?
Green builds you can believe.
Feedback in minutes, not days.
Room to raise risk early without getting punished for it.
Celebrating the bug that never shipped, louder than the overnight hero fix.
And shared ownership: if you change it, you test it.

At Rocket Rez, I'd aim for boring releases when the park is slammed.
A.I. can help draft cases and spot gaps.
The call about the guest still belongs to the team.

Happy to dig in live.
Thanks for the look.
